**Introduction** : In this project, I am addressing the problem of automatically detecting 20 thoracic pathologies from Chest X-ray images. This is a multi-class classification task where each image must be mapped to exactly one disease category.I believe this problem is critical because, in a clinical environment, the cost of a "False Negative" (missing a life-threatening condition) is far higher than a "False Positive" (a false alarm). This reality is reflected in our dataset's scoring, where a missed diagnosis carries a -5 penalty. My dataset consists of approximately 51,000 training images, which are heavily imbalanced toward "No Finding." My primary objective is to build a model that is "pathology-sensitive," prioritizing the detection of rare diseases to minimize these heavy penalties while maintaining a high macro-averaged score.
**Methodology** : For my architecture, I chose EfficientNet-B0 and utilized Transfer Learning with ImageNet weights. I selected EfficientNet because its compound scaling allows it to capture fine-grained medical textures much better than a standard ResNet, while still being efficient enough to run on my available hardware.My preprocessing pipeline involves resizing images to 224x224 and applying Normalization based on the ImageNet mean and standard deviation. To make my model more robust, I used Data Augmentation techniques like Horizontal Flips and 5° Rotations. I purposefully avoided vertical flips because they would create anatomically impossible heart and lung orientations.To handle the -5 penalty, I implemented an Asymmetric Weighted Loss. I modified the Cross-Entropy loss so that if the model misses a disease, the error is multiplied by 5.0. Finally, during inference, I applied a 0.85 multiplier to the "No Finding" probability, effectively lowering the barrier for the model to flag a potential pathology.
**Experiments** : I began my experiments by comparing a standard 4-layer CNN against EfficientNet-B0. The simple CNN struggled with the class imbalance, whereas EfficientNet showed a much stronger ability to generalize.I also experimented with different Learning Rates, finding that $1 \times 10^{-4}$ was the "sweet spot" for stable convergence. I explored various Batch Sizes, eventually settling on 32 to maximize GPU utility without triggering "Out of Memory" errors. My validation strategy relied on a 90/10 Train-Validation split, which allowed me to monitor if my loss weighting was actually reducing False Negatives on unseen data. I observed that without the 5x weight, the model would lazily predict "No Finding" for almost everything to achieve high accuracy, but the weighted loss forced it to actually learn the disease features.
**Results** :Throughout my experimental phase, I observed a clear correlation between cost-sensitive loss weighting and my final competition score. My baseline EfficientNet-B0 model, trained with standard Cross-Entropy loss, achieved high overall accuracy but suffered a poor weighted penalty score of -5.12 because it frequently missed rare pathologies in favor of the "No Finding" class. By introducing the 5x loss weighting, I saw my validation score improve to -4.45, as the model became significantly more "cautious" and started identifying subtle disease features it previously ignored. The most substantial breakthrough occurred when I combined the weighted loss with a 0.85 inference bias on the "No Finding" class; this final strategy yielded my best result of -4.18. This confirmed that mathematically tilting the decision boundary toward "disease" was the most effective way to avoid the heavy -5 penalty inherent in the competition's scoring logic.
**Conclusion** : My final approach centered on bridging the gap between standard image classification and clinical risk management. By utilizing a pre-trained EfficientNet-B0 backbone, I leveraged high-level visual features while focusing my training effort on an asymmetric cost-sensitive loss. This strategy specifically penalized the omission of diseases by fivefold, forcing the model to prioritize sensitivity over simple accuracy. I further refined this by introducing a post-processing bias during inference, which strategically shifted the decision threshold to favor pathology detection in ambiguous cases. The key learning I took away from this project is that "accuracy" can be a misleading metric when the consequences of different errors are unequal. I learned that deep learning models are highly malleable; by adjusting the cost function and decision boundaries, I can align a model's behavior with specific real-world penalties. Furthermore, I discovered that addressing extreme class imbalance requires more than just high-quality data—it requires an intentional algorithmic bias to ensure that rare but critical classes are not ignored by the optimizer.
